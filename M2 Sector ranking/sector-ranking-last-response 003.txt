## Economic Cycle Positioning

The economy is in **Late Cycle** transition, tipping toward **Falling Growth + Rising Inflation (Stagflation)**.

The evidence is unambiguous. GDP growth collapsed from 4.38% annualized in Q3 2025 to just 0.65% in Q4 2025—a deceleration sharp enough to raise recession risk. At the same time, Core PCE inflation accelerated from 3.00% in December to 3.10% in January, and CPI held at 2.43% in February, both well above the Fed's 2% target. WTI crude oil jumped 9.3% in a single session to $95.35 per barrel, adding fuel to inflationary pressures. The ISM Manufacturing PMI fell from 52.4 to 51.6, signaling that factory activity is still expanding but losing momentum. The unemployment rate ticked up from 4.30% to 4.40%, a small but notable softening in labor markets. The yield curve remains positive at +0.55%, which typically signals expansion, but the sharp rise in both short-term (2Y up 17 bps) and long-term (10Y up 13 bps) yields suggests bond investors are pricing in either persistent inflation or a policy mistake by the Fed.

**Stagflation** is the term for an economy where growth slows or stalls while prices keep rising—a toxic combination that leaves the Federal Reserve with no good options. Cutting rates to support growth risks fueling more inflation; holding rates steady risks tipping the economy into recession. This regime historically favors assets that hold value when currencies weaken (commodities, gold) and punishes assets that depend on expanding profit margins (cyclical stocks, long-duration bonds).

## Macro Themes

### Theme 1: Stagflation Risk Is No Longer Theoretical

**Thesis:** The U.S. economy is exhibiting the early hallmarks of stagflation—growth collapsing while inflation remains elevated—a regime that has not been priced into equity markets since the 1970s. GDP growth fell from 4.38% annualized in Q3 2025 to just 0.65% in Q4 2025, a deceleration severe enough to raise recession risk. At the same time, Core PCE inflation accelerated from 3.00% to 3.10%, and oil spiked 9.3% in a single session to $95.35 per barrel. The Fed is trapped: cutting rates to support growth risks re-igniting inflation, while holding rates steady risks tipping the economy into contraction. This is the worst possible environment for traditional 60/40 portfolios, which rely on bonds rallying when stocks fall—a correlation that breaks down when inflation is the dominant force.

**Human stakes:** For businesses, this means input costs (energy, labor, materials) keep rising even as customer demand weakens, crushing profit margins. For workers, it means wages may rise nominally but fall in real terms as prices outpace paychecks. For retirees holding bonds, it means their fixed income loses purchasing power every month inflation stays elevated.

**Evidence:** GDP growth 0.65% annualized (Q4 2025), down from 4.38% (Q3 2025). Core PCE inflation 3.10% (January 2026), up from 3.00% (December 2025). WTI crude oil $95.35/bbl, up 9.3% in one session (March 16, 2026). ISM Manufacturing PMI 51.6 (February 2026), down from 52.4 (January 2026), signaling slowing factory activity.

**Portfolio Implication:** Overweight Energy (benefits from rising oil prices), Utilities (inflation pass-through and bond-proxy characteristics), and gold (classic inflation hedge). Underweight long-duration growth stocks (Technology, Consumer Discretionary) whose valuations depend on low discount rates and expanding multiples.
