## Macro Themes

### Theme 1: Stagflation Risk Is No Longer Theoretical

**Theme Statement:** The U.S. economy is exhibiting the classic symptoms of stagflation — growth collapsing while inflation accelerates — a combination that has historically been toxic for risk assets and forces a defensive sector rotation.

**Thesis:** GDP growth fell from 4.38% annualized in Q3 2025 to just 0.65% in Q4, a deceleration so sharp it suggests either a technical recession or the edge of one. At the same time, Core PCE inflation rose to 3.10% and oil spiked 9.28% in a single session to $95.35 per barrel. This is not a transitory shock — it is the intersection of exhausted fiscal stimulus, persistent supply-side inflation (energy, labor), and a Fed that cannot cut rates without risking an inflation re-acceleration. Stagflation environments punish cyclical sectors (Industrials, Discretionary, Financials) because earnings collapse while input costs rise, compressing margins. They reward sectors with pricing power and inelastic demand (Healthcare, Utilities, Staples) because those businesses can pass costs through to customers without losing volume.

**Human stakes:** A small-business owner who sells furniture or appliances is getting squeezed from both sides: fewer customers are walking through the door because the economy is slowing, but the cost of lumber, steel, and shipping keeps rising because oil is expensive and supply chains are still tight. That business cannot raise prices without losing the few customers it has left — so margins collapse. Meanwhile, a hospital or a utility company can raise prices in line with inflation because people cannot choose to skip chemotherapy or turn off the electricity. That asymmetry is what makes defensive sectors work in stagflation.

**Evidence:** Q4 2025 GDP growth of 0.65% annualized versus 4.38% in Q3 (a 3.73 percentage point deceleration). Core PCE inflation at 3.10% in January 2026, up from 3.00% in December 2025. WTI crude oil at $95.35 per barrel as of March 16, 2026, up from $87.25 the prior session. ISM Manufacturing PMI at 51.6 in February 2026, down from 52.4 in January, signaling deceleration in the goods-producing economy.

**Portfolio implication:** Overweight Healthcare, Utilities, and Consumer Staples; underweight Industrials, Consumer Discretionary, and Financials. Within Healthcare, prioritize pharmaceutical companies with patent-protected drugs and pricing power over hospitals and insurers — drug companies can raise prices annually without regulatory pushback in most therapeutic categories, while hospitals face reimbursement pressure. Within Utilities, favor regulated electric utilities with rate-base growth over merchant power generators — regulated utilities earn a fixed return on invested capital regardless of economic conditions, while merchant generators are exposed to volatile power prices.

### Theme 2: The Oil Shock Is a Policy Dilemma, Not Just a Price Move

**Theme Statement:** Oil's 9.28% single-session spike to $95.35 per barrel forces the Fed into an impossible choice — cut rates to support growth and risk re-igniting inflation, or hold rates steady and accept a deeper slowdown.

**Thesis:** Oil at $95 per barrel feeds directly into gasoline prices, which are the most visible and politically sensitive component of inflation. Every $10 increase in oil adds roughly 25 cents per gallon at the pump, and consumers respond by cutting discretionary spending elsewhere. This creates a negative feedback loop: higher oil → lower consumer spending → weaker GDP → rising unemployment → weaker corporate earnings. Normally, the Fed would cut rates to cushion the blow. But Core PCE inflation is already at 3.10%, well above the 2% target, and cutting rates now would signal that the Fed is abandoning the inflation fight — which could un-anchor inflation expectations and make the problem worse. The Fed is likely to stay on hold, which means the economy absorbs the full deflationary impact of the oil shock without monetary offset.

**Human stakes:** A family that spends $200 per month on gasoline suddenly spends $250. That extra $50 comes out of somewhere else — fewer restaurant meals, delayed car repairs, skipped vacations. Multiply that across millions of households, and entire sectors (restaurants, hotels, retail) see demand evaporate. The Fed's job is to decide whether to make borrowing cheaper to offset that pain — but doing so risks making inflation permanent.

**Evidence:** WTI crude oil at $95.35 per barrel on March 16, 2026, up 9.28% in a single session. Core PCE inflation at 3.10% in January 2026. Fed Funds Rate unchanged at 3.50-3.75% despite GDP growth collapsing to 0.65% annualized. Treasury yields rising (2Y up 17 bps, 10Y up 13 bps) as the market prices out rate cuts.

**Portfolio implication:** Overweight Energy as a tactical inflation hedge, but be selective — favor integrated oil majors (companies that both produce crude oil and refine it into gasoline and diesel) over pure-play exploration and production names. Integrated majors capture the margin between crude input costs and refined product prices, which widens when oil spikes, and they have diversified revenue streams that cushion downside risk if oil reverses. Within Energy, prioritize companies with domestic refining exposure and low debt-to-EBITDA ratios (below 1.5x) — high leverage becomes dangerous if oil volatility triggers a credit crunch.

**Thesis:** The ISM Services PMI at 56.1 in February 2026 (up from 53.8 in January) signals that the consumer-facing economy — restaurants, healthcare, software, entertainment — is still healthy. A PMI above 50 means the sector is expanding, and 56.1 is well above that threshold. Meanwhile, the ISM Manufacturing PMI at 51.6 (down from 52.4) is barely expanding and decelerating. This split reflects two forces: first, the U.S. consumer is still employed (jobless claims at 213K, near cycle lows) and still spending on services, even as goods spending slows; second, manufacturing is more exposed to global trade, commodity costs, and inventory cycles, all of which are under pressure. Historically, when services outperform manufacturing by this margin, it signals that the economy is late-cycle and consumer-driven sectors outperform industrial sectors.

**Human stakes:** A software company that sells cloud subscriptions to businesses is thriving — its customers are still paying their monthly bills, and the company's costs (mostly labor and data centers) are relatively stable. A steel mill, by contrast, is struggling — demand for steel from construction and auto manufacturing is falling, but the cost of energy and raw materials is rising because oil is expensive. The steel mill cannot raise prices without losing orders to cheaper imports, so margins collapse.

**Evidence:** ISM Services PMI at 56.1 in February 2026, up from 53.8 in January. ISM Manufacturing PMI at 51.6 in February 2026, down from 52.4 in January. Initial jobless claims at 213K as of March 7, 2026, near cycle lows. Unemployment rate at 4.40% in February 2026, up modestly from 4.30% but still historically low.

**Portfolio implication:** Within Technology, overweight enterprise software and cloud infrastructure names with subscription revenue models, gross margins above 70%, and customer retention rates above 95% — these businesses have pricing power and are insulated from consumer weakness. Underweight semiconductor and hardware names, which are more exposed to manufacturing cycles and inventory destocking. Within Consumer Discretionary, favor services over goods — restaurants, travel, and entertainment over apparel and home furnishings. Avoid Industrials and Materials broadly, with the exception of copper-exposed miners (copper benefits from long-term electrification trends and offers partial inflation protection).
